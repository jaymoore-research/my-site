Thoughts on AI biosecurity, discovery, and science's track record of self-regulation

All words here are written by me without AI and views shared are my own.

Recently I attended an informal roundtable event on biosecurity. Although I'm a keen advocate for AI adoption in science, hosting events and workshops, I am starting to understand that it is essential for researchers and the public to understand biosecurity. Attending this event highlighted how diverse views of the risks were. To begin learning about biosecurity, and the risk of AI adoption, I compiled a historic timeline of the biosecurity and biosafety risks in biology.

The timeline follows discovery milestones, and events that changed the regulation and oversight landscape for that discovery. The view is not intended to be extensive, and I limited the timeline to mostly UK and US regulations.

The visualisation follows:
* discovery before oversight (grey),
* nonbinding guidance/self-regulation (green)
* oversight (blue)
* ban, voluntary pause or funding restriction (red if instated in at least one country)
* green diamonds mark illustrative discoveries and treatments.

My expectations going into this were:
1) discoveries marred by controversies and risky research become unpopular and progress slowly
2) research in fields with strong regulation move slower and lack innovation
3) dangerous individuals are a risk to science regardless of regulatory environments

Key examples (worth detailed follow up)

•	The Asilomar Conference on Recombinant DNA (Genetic engineering, 1975). Concerns were raised about biosecurity risk of recombinant DNA research due to its risks to infect people with E. coli containing viral genomes. Paul Berg, who received a Nobel Prize for his contributions to this work, helped organise the Asilomar Conference on Recombinant DNA to develop guidelines for this work (from Summary statement of the Asilomar conference on recombinant DNA molecules, P Berg, D Baltimore, S Brenner, R O Roblin, M F Singer, 1975):
    * “i) containment be made an essential consideration in the experimental design”
    * “ii) that the effectiveness of the containment should match ... the estimated risk”
    * "Estimating the risks will be difficult and intuitive at first but this will improve as we acquire additional knowledge"

* Even within periods of funding restrictions, innovation can occur. The discovery of iPSCs, during a funding restriction of hESCs, opened up new fields (personalised medicine) and safer technologies (iPSC-based cell therapy).


* Other discoveries ran on trust first: IVF was licensed voluntarily by the profession for six years before statutory licensing began in 1991. While other discoveries have included examples of breaking of rules and trust: CRISPR editing of germline genome of two babies born in China by scientist He Jiankui (Heritability editing, 2018).

AI's role in biosecurity

AI's role in biosecurity is not fully answered when viewed as a risk on its own. AI-lab automation is a burgeoning field within "Research practice and safety", and the connection of AI to biology (including automation) can be viewed as a rapid accelerator that will affect all fields.

Coming from the history of innovation and regulation, it appears that biosecurity and biosafety as they have been installed cannot prevent all mistakes, even amongst researchers and institutions with good intentions. As AI increasingly enters the lab (https://www.nature.com/articles/d41586-026-03270-1) through consumer frontier LLMs or purpose-built AI for biology products, high levels of scrutiny are going to be needed to ensure AI-driven research is well aligned and safe. Adoption of tools is needed to build this safer biology research:

- Setting boundaries for materials and products: for example BioBricks (iGEM), where synthetic biology works with predetermined units of defined DNA sequences to prevent creation of dangerous organisms/infections
- AI-Lab automation. Traceable, controlled, and well-governed automated research provides a clear path to accountability in science. Frameworks like Design-Build-Test-Learn (DBTL) are being used in foundries to optimise bioengineering.
- Improved interpretability of AI biology foundation models. A good example here is of Goodfire's analysis of the Prima Mente's Pleiades epigenetic foundation model https://www.goodfire.com/research/interpretability-for-alzheimers-detection.
- Using AI risk management practices including evaluation of real world effectiveness and incident reporting https://internationalaisafetyreport.org/sites/default/files/2026-02/international-ai-safety-report-2026.pdf
- Know Your Customer (KYC) are "laws, regulations and guidelines that require regulated businesses and professionals to verify the identity of a customer". Accountability is needed to trace follow research intent and outcomes.

Wrap up

Considering the biosecurity and biosafety risks that already exist today is helpful for framing AI for biology:

* AI is entering research now, and has limited safeguards.
* We have seen that science has handled discovery risks through a mix of self-regulation, regulations and guidelines.
* We need to develop tools quickly to manage AI entering fields already with risks, this may come in the form of guidelines, software, models and lab automation.
