Thoughts on AI biosecurity, discovery, and science's track record of self-regulation

All words here are written by me without AI and views share are my own.

Biosecurity and AI is an interesting topics because it is difficult to separate lived experience of the global pandemic, from existential discussion over the risk of AI super intelligence. I've been a big supported of AI adoption in AI science, hosting events and teaching workshops on the topic, but as with most things, it can be worth to consider to benefit and risks to what you are doing. With that, one topic that followed me after the event was - what are the risks in biology and how do they change with AI? To start, I wanted to take a look at discoveries in biology and collate our experiences with responding to the challenge of navigating risk, innovation and regulation in science.

History of science regulation

I compiled a timeline, following from discovery milestones, self-regulation and oversight, as well as tracking mistakes and harm caused. The generated timeline goes through research following from discovery before oversight (grey), nonbinding guidance/self-regulation (green), oversight (blue), and a at least one research restriction (ban, voluntary pause or funding restriction) in a country (red). Green diamonds mark illustrative discoveries and treatments. The view is not intended to be extensive, and I limited the timeline to mostly UK and US regulations. Finally, I've include AI lab automation as its own timeline, but it should also be viewed as impacting all of these tracks by enabling faster research. My expectations going into this were:
1) discoveries marred by controversies and risky research become unpopular and progress slowly
2) research in fields with strong regulation more slower and lack innovation
3) dangerous individuals are a risk to science regardless of regulatory environments

We see that the discovery of genetics in the 1960's has sparked a lot of new technologies and fields in biology. Some follow quite a clean narrative: recombinant DNA for instance: pause, self-regulation, oversight, then innovative treatment. The [Asilomar Conference on Recombinant DNA](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/sites/books/NBK195888/) (Genetic engineering, 1975), is great example of pausing research to define the regulations. Concerns were raised about biosecurity risks that splicing DNA across viruses and trapping them in the genome of bacteria may infect humans, causing cancer. [Paul Berg](https://www.nsf.gov/honorary-awards/national-medal-science/recipients/paul-berg), who received a Nobel Prize for his contributions to this work, helped organise the [Asilomar Conference on Recombinant DNA](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/sites/books/NBK195888/) to develop guidelines with the NIH for this work. Approval and commercialisation of a [engineered bacteria-derived insulin](https://www.fda.gov/about-fda/fda-history-exhibits/100-years-insulin) in was delivered in 1982.

Others are more trust based: [IVF moving from voluntary management, to oversight](https://www.hfea.gov.uk/about-us/news-and-press-releases/2018/ivf-regulation-ensuring-safe-and-ethical-treatment/) to continued work. And then of course a break in trust with the [CRISPR editing of germline genome of two babies born in China](https://www.nationalacademies.org/news/statement-by-the-organizing-committee-of-the-second-international-summit-on-human-genome-editing) by scientist JK (Heritability editing, 2018). Innovations can also occur during periods of funding restrictions. The [discovery of iPSCs](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18035408/), during a [funding restriction hESCs](https://www.nih.gov/sites/default/files/about-nih/nih-director/articles/collins/steering-a-new-course.pdf) points to another dynamic that regulation may promote innovation.

AI-Bio's role in biosecurity

AI-Bio's role in biosecurity is not fully answered when viewed as a risk on its won. AI-Bio is both a burgeoning field within "Research practice and safety", and a rapid accelerator that will effect all fields. It is clear across the other fields of biology however, that there is a patchwork of voluntary self-regulation, strict oversight and funding restrictions. It is not obvious to point the role of Asilomar Conference on this final clinical success.

What is clear is that AI is highly empowering everyone to deal in topics of high complexity. I think this will begin to breakdown the barrier around science in the act of doing biology research and making products. Going from the history of science, it is clear that mistakes in biosecurity and biosafety can happen by groups and institutions within the science as well. Good-natured, cure-searching science should also be held to high scrutiny when using AI to ensure is well aligned and safe. The tools to build this safer realm of biology research during the emergence of AI is starting to be built:

- [BioBricks (iGEM)](https://technology.igem.org/registry), where synthetic biology works with predetermined units of defined DNA sequences, setting boundaries on materials used and products.
- AI-Lab automation. Traceable, controlled, and well-governed automated research provides a clear path to accountability in science. Frameworks like [Design-Build-Test-Learn (DBTL)](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-019-10079-2) are being used in foundries to optimise bioengineering.
- Improved interpretability of AI-Bio models. A good example here is of [Goodfire's analysis of the Prima Mente's Pleiades epigenetic foundation model](https://www.goodfire.com/research/interpretability-for-alzheimers-detection).
- Using [AI risk management practices including evaluation of real world effectiveness and incident reporting](https://internationalaisafetyreport.org/sites/default/files/2026-02/international-ai-safety-report-2026.pdf)
- [Know Your Customer (KYC)](https://www.finra.org/rules-guidance/rulebooks/finra-rules/2090) are "laws, regulations and guidelines that require regulated businesses and professionals to verify the identity of a customer". Accountability is needed to trace problems.

Wrap up

In 2017, [Beneficial AI 2017 conference](https://futureoflife.org/event/bai-2017/) met at the same Asilomar Conference Grounds to discuss rapidly development of AI. Like always, discussion and conversation remain core to how we operate as a research community. As our ambitions in science grow, so do the risks but I'm optimistic that we have many of the ideas and regulations are already in motion, and AI-Bio will be able to safely be incorporated into the scientific pantheon.
